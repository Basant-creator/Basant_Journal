§1

The surveyor, not the outlaw

The frontier genre holds two figures. One is the outlaw — guns, saloons, wanted posters, memes. The other is the surveyor: the person who arrived with instruments, walked the ground, measured it, drew it, and wrote it down. Both are period-accurate. Only one is about rigour, observation and documentation — and that one maps exactly onto engineering. Choosing the surveyor removes every cowboy cliché without needing a rule against any of them.

Open decision · carried to Phase 2

Cinzel is a placeholder worth replacing. It is Roman inscriptional — it reads classical and luxurious, closer to a perfume campaign than a frontier survey. Compare it in-browser at real sizes against IM Fell English SC (genuinely period, with the ink-spread irregularity the concept wants) and a condensed slab in the Zilla Slab / Bitter family (closer to letterpress, and stronger in the professional view). The token name does not change — only its value.

And the wordmark itself gets drawn, not set. Custom SVG lettering makes the identity type provably Basant's own — which also settles the “no game fonts” question permanently.

§13–14

Motion budget & access floor

Motion communicates arrival, exploration, discovery and transition. Nothing animates to be impressive. The test that matters: with every animation switched off, the composition must still feel deliberate — if it only works animated, the composition is wrong.

TWO AT A TIMEAt most two elements animate simultaneously, outside the single choreographed map reveal.
ONCE, THEN DONEEntry animations run once per session. Re-scrolling a section never replays it.
NOTHING LOOPSNo floating cards, no pulsing nodes, no breathing glows. The only continuous motion is dust — the first thing switched off.
NEVER GATEDText is in the DOM and selectable before its reveal finishes. If JavaScript fails, everything is visible.
SKIPPABLEAny click, key or scroll completes a reveal immediately. No loading screens of any kind, including “cinematic” ones.
TRANSFORM & OPACITYOnly. No animated layout, width, height or filter. Parallax capped at ~20px of travel.
GREYSCALE TESTView the site desaturated. Every state — current page, visited node, travelled trail — must still be distinguishable.
ONE COMPOSITE MAPOne tab stop; arrow keys traverse locations in trail order; Escape returns to the persistent nav.
SURFACE-SCOPED FOCUSPaper-light ring on dark, ink ring on paper. One global ring colour would be invisible on exactly the surfaces holding the most content.
16px FLOORBody text never smaller, on any breakpoint. Layout survives 200% zoom and 400% text-only zoom.
44px TARGETSEvery interactive element on touch, with hit area independent of visual size.
TEXTURE YIELDSIf the fibre layer drops any pairing below 4.5:1, the texture opacity comes down — never the text contrast.
§15

Inspiration, not imitation

The design borrows a genre and an atmosphere — both 19th-century visual culture, both in the public domain, both belonging to no one. It borrows nothing any company owns.

BORROWED

The genre · public domain

  • Aged survey maps and hand-drawn cartography
  • Frontier and letterpress display typography
  • Field journals and marginalia
  • Campfire and camp imagery
  • Sepia and wet-plate collodion photography
  • Dusty landscapes, worn paper, muted earth palettes
  • Exploration as structure; chapter-based storytelling

NOT BORROWED

The product · owned

  • Any logo, wordmark or brand identity
  • The game's UI, menus or HUD
  • Its exact typefaces — ours is drawn, not set
  • Any character, gang, place or faction name
  • Screenshots or recognisable in-game locations
  • Copied layouts or interaction patterns
  • Any extracted, traced or “placeholder” game asset

The stranger test

Show it to someone who has never played the game. They should find it atmospheric, coherent and well-made, with no sense of missing a reference. If an element only makes sense as a reference, it is a fan-site element and it goes.

The takedown test

If Rockstar's legal team looked at this, would there be anything to point at? There must be nothing — no asset, no mark, no character, no copied interface. Atmosphere is not ownable. Assets are.

The final impression this document exists to produce: “This is an unusually memorable portfolio, and the person behind it can actually build things.” Where atmosphere and technical clarity conflict, clarity wins — every time.
